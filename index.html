How it works

The 1% line

Ingredients above 1% have to be listed from most to least. That means the ingredient at position n can't be more than 100 ÷ n percent, because the ones before it are at least as big and together they can't pass 100.

at most = 100 ÷ position

Below 1%, brands can list ingredients in any order. Some ingredients are almost never used above 1%: preservatives like phenoxyethanol, thickeners like xanthan gum, EDTA, fragrance. The first one on the list draws the line, and everything from there down is capped at 1%. Each hero ingredient's cap is then compared with the amount studies use. Niacinamide at 1% or less is not doing what 5% does. Retinol and hyaluronic acid are different: they work below 1%, so they sit low on a list and that's fine.

The data

Every ingredient in the EU's CosIng database, 33,639 of them, downloaded from the search API behind CosIng's own site. The API stops at 10,000 results per query, and its range filters don't work on the ID field because the IDs are stored as text. So the download asks for IDs in explicit batches of 5,000, pages through each one, and checks the total against the API's own count.

Reading a messy label

A comma ends a name, except in chemistry (1,2-Hexanediol) and inside brackets. One ingredient can be printed three ways at once (Aqua/Water/Eau, Parfum (Fragrance)). Footnotes like *Organic are dropped, and the "may contain [+/-]" shade colorants are kept apart because they sit outside the order.

Each name is matched in three passes: everyday names (Water, Vitamin C), then exact INCI names, then typos. For typos, a trigram index narrows 33,000 names to 40 candidates and Damerau-Levenshtein edit distance picks the closest, so Glycrein reads as Glycerin. Anything further than about one typo per eight letters stays unknown rather than being guessed.

Fragrance-free?

Three things give a scent away: Parfum or Fragrance, essential oils, and the 26 fragrance allergens EU law makes brands name (linalool, limonene, citronellol…). A "fragrance-free" product with lavender oil and linalool on it is not fragrance-free. Benzyl alcohol is on the allergen list but is usually the preservative, so on its own it doesn't count.

What base is this foundation?

The base is the part that touches your skin, not the biggest ingredient. Most liquid foundations are water-in-silicone and still list water first, at 40–60% of the bottle. So the decoder reads the top eight ingredients and the emulsifier, and checks eight rules in order:

RuleWhat it looks forBase
1No water anywhereWaterless
2Water near the top, a silicone in the top four, a silicone emulsifierSilicone
3A silicone before the waterSilicone
4Oils or evaporating hydrocarbons up top, a water-in-oil emulsifier, no siliconeOil
5Silicones and oils sharing the top fiveSilicone + oil
6Water first, nothing silicone or oily up top, water-phase thickenersWater
7Water first, silicones in it, thickened like a gelWater (a guess)
8Nothing fitsUnknown, with what it saw

The rules come from my research on foundation chemistry and on whether oil-based products belong in hot, humid places like Austin in August: read the report.

Can I use these together?

The clashes live in one table as data: retinoids with AHAs or BHAs, retinoids or vitamin C with benzoyl peroxide, copper peptides with vitamin C or acids. A clash only counts when the active is there in an amount that works, so lactic acid at the bottom of a list, adjusting the pH, isn't treated as an exfoliant. Clashes inside one product don't count: the chemist already made those work.
