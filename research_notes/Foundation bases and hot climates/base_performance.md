# Water-, Silicone- and Oil-Based Foundations: Real-World Performance

Scope note: "water-based", "silicone-based" and "oil-based" are commercial shorthand, not strict chemical categories. Most liquid foundations are emulsions that contain water. What decides how a foundation behaves is the **continuous (outer) phase** (water, silicone or oil), the **film formers or resins**, and the **powders/fillers**. Published peer-reviewed data comparing the three "bases" head to head is very thin. Most rigorous work comes from ingredient suppliers (Dow Corning, L'Oréal) and looks at film formers and sebum rather than at "water vs silicone vs oil" labels.

## Q1. What do instrumental or clinical studies show about longevity, transfer and sebum interaction by formulation type?

### Takeaway
Sebum is the main thing that breaks down foundation over the day. It makes the film softer, so more colour transfers and rubs off, and it darkens the shade. Long wear comes mostly from rigid silicone resins or film formers plus surface-treated pigments and oil-absorbing fillers, not from the "base" label alone. Most "12/24-hour" figures are small brand-run clinical claims.

### Cited Findings
- **Labels vs chemistry:** a cosmetic chemist quoted by NewBeauty says "water-based" and "silicone-based" are handy commercial terms, not strict scientific categories. Silicone-based foundations usually still contain water, often listed first. A practical way to tell them apart is whether "-cone" ingredients come right after water — [NewBeauty](https://www.newbeauty.com/view/silicone-based-vs-water-based-makeup)
- **Typical composition:** water-in-oil/silicone emulsions are the most popular foundation type. They hold about 40–60% water, 10–20% silicones (cyclopentasiloxane, dimethicone) and 3–15% TiO2. They "apply more evenly and feel better on skin" because oil stays in continuous contact with the skin. Oil-in-water types are less common now, and powders are anhydrous (mica, talc) — [George Deckner, UL Prospector, "Contemporary Foundation Formulations"](https://ulprospector.ul.com/knowledge/7389/pcc-contemporary-foundation-formulations/)
- **Dow Corning's "Soft + Hard" model (J. Cosmet. Sci. 68(1), 2017):** long wear depends on the ratio of "hard" non-volatiles (resins, particulates, solid emulsifiers) to "soft" non-volatiles (non-volatile oils, liquid emulsifiers). Too much soft material caused rub-off, and an all-hard film was brittle and cracked. The test methods were:
  - a flexibility/stretch test
  - an abrasion test: 100 rubs on collagen substrate, with and without artificial sebum, colour transfer measured by colorimetry
  - a contact-transfer test on nylon filter paper
  — [Journal of Cosmetic Science / SCC library](https://library.scconline.org/v068n01/94)
- **Sebum effect, same study:** pretreating with artificial sebum sharply increased pigment transfer in every formulation tested. When sebum mixes with the non-volatile components, it lowers the effective hard:soft ratio and plasticises the film, so pigment binds less well and transfers more. Sebum resistance was best when the film components were compatible with each other and immiscible with sebum. Among water-in-silicone foundations, the emulsifier that was compatible with silicone acrylate and least miscible with sebum gave the best sebum resistance — [J. Cosmet. Sci. 68(1)](https://library.scconline.org/v068n01/94); [related abstract](https://library.scconline.org/v068n01/95)
- **L'Oréal physical characterisation of long-wear foundations:** Bui, Ebanks and Hasebe tested Estée Lauder Double Wear, Revlon ColorStay and Lancôme Teint Idole Ultra. Methods included:
  - contact angle and surface energy of the dried films, with artificial sebum, sweat and water droplets
  - optical analysis of film integrity after 8 hours of exposure to sebum and sweat
  - rheology and tribology

  Results: performance under sebum and sweat depended on both the film former/resin and the choice of filler. Long-wear foundations typically pair silicone resins or silicone film formers with fillers — [ECI Digital Archives, Bui et al.](https://dc.engconfintl.org/cosmetic/23)
- **Darkening/oxidation:** an SCC study reports that sebum is the biggest cause of liquid foundation darkening, and that sweat and light matter less. Surface-treated pigments and fillers may reduce sebum-driven darkening — [SCC study, "Factors Affecting the Darkening of Liquid Foundations" (PDF)](https://www.scconline.org/Portals/100/SCC-Study%20On%20the%20Factors%20Affecting%20The%20Darkening%20of%20liquid%20Foundations.pdf). Caveat: I could not extract the full text; this finding comes from the search-result abstract.
- **Objective transfer testing:** the Journal of Cosmetic Science notes that transfer resistance matters to consumers but few objective methods for measuring it had been published — [SCC library](https://library.scconline.org/v051n05/57)
- **Sebum-absorbing powders:** a patent describes a five-hour on-face photo test in which a powder foundation with a sebum-adsorbent powder lasted better and showed less shine than controls. This is patent data, not peer-reviewed — [US Patent 6730309](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6730309). A Dow/industry patent covers "transfer-resistant and long wear foundation in emulsion form containing oil absorbing powders" — [US Patent 9308396](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9308396)
- **Brand clinical claims (marketing only, small samples, not independently replicated):**
  - NARS: 24-hour shine control, n=33 — [NARS HK](https://www.narscosmetics.com.hk/en/soft-matte-advanced-perfecting-powder/0194251136066_hk.html?cgid=face)
  - bareMinerals BAREPRO: 12-hour wear; "100%" of 32 women reported reduced oiliness over 6 weeks — [Dermstore](https://www.Dermstore.com/bareminerals-barepro-foundation-10g-various-shades/11331569.html)
  - Mary Kay: 12 hours, n=20 — [Mary Kay](https://www.marykay.com/klavorata/en-us/products/makeup/face/foundation/110351)

### Inferences
- Water-in-silicone foundations with resins such as trimethylsiloxysilicate or silicone acrylates are the long-wear, transfer-resistant category in the literature. This is because the film resists sebum, not because "silicone" is magic.
- Oil-based (anhydrous oil or oil-continuous) foundations should be the least sebum-resistant. Sebum mixes readily with the oils, which shifts the film toward "soft" and increases transfer and breakdown. This is mechanistic reasoning from the Dow model, not a direct comparative study.
- Mostly-water (oil-in-water) foundations dry to a thin film once the water evaporates. Their wear depends on any polymer film formers and powders, which usually means lighter coverage and less transfer resistance than resin-heavy water-in-silicone formulas. Evidence is indirect.
- Finish follows the same logic. Volatile silicones plus high powder or filler load give matte. Non-volatile oils, glycerin and humectants give dewy. Satin sits in between. No peer-reviewed study ties finish to base type.

### Gaps
- I found no independent peer-reviewed study comparing water-, silicone- and oil-based foundations on wear hours, transfer, or sweat and water resistance in humans.
- I found no quantitative sweat or humidity data broken down by base type. The L'Oréal work covers sweat solutions only qualitatively in its abstract.
- I could not read the full text of the SCC darkening study or the IFSCC 2022 foundation paper (the latter returned 403).

## Q2. Is "match water with water, silicone with silicone" supported by chemists? What actually causes pilling and separation?

### Takeaway
The rule is a simplification with some truth in it. Chemists explain pilling and separation by **outer-phase incompatibility, layer quantity and drying time, film-forming polymers and thickeners (e.g. carbomer), skin condition, and rubbing**. The one large human study found that sunscreen, not foundation, drives most pilling. Even chemists disagree on the direction of the mismatch, so "test it on your skin" is the honest advice.

### Cited Findings
- **Lab Muffin (Michelle Wong, cosmetic chemist):** clumping happens "when the outer phases of their formulas don't match." Most foundations are water-in-silicone emulsions, so a water-in-silicone foundation over a silicone-in-water product can repel and separate. Other causes she lists:
  - sebum produced during wear disrupts the film
  - unstable emulsions break during application
  - some oils don't mix with silicones

  Her fixes: test combinations, let layers dry so the water evaporates, use less product underneath, choose thicker and more stable foundations, use polymer setting sprays, or apply a light powder layer under foundation. She bases this on emulsion principles, with no controlled experiment — [Lab Muffin Beauty Science](https://labmuffin.com/fixing-foundation-clumping-with-video/)
- **Counterpoint (Javon Ford, cosmetic chemist):** most primers are water-based while most foundations are silicone-based, yet silicone foundations "tend to play well" with water-based primers. Problems arise more with a water-based foundation over a slippery silicone primer — [Airmail, "Don't Be a Pill" (2024)](https://airmail.news/look/issues/2024-10-4/dont-be-a-pill)
- **Largest empirical study (Skin Research and Technology, 2024; Lua, Ruan, Lyu, Liu):**
  - Design: 528 Chinese women aged 20–49, 10 base formulas plus sunscreen and foundation, 12 facial sites. Instruments: Sebumeter, Corneometer, pH meter, C-Cube texture analyser.
  - 80% of pilling events happened when sunscreen was applied over a base product: 655 events during sunscreen application vs 35 with foundation.
  - People who pilled had lower skin hydration, lower sebum, higher pH and smoother texture.
  - Rubbing caused the most pilling (circular 266, linear 154 events) compared with patting or pressing.
  - Applying foundation resolved sunscreen-induced pilling in 98.9% of cases.

  — [PMC11294729](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11294729/)
- **Thickeners:** carbomer is often blamed for pilling with silicones or minerals, especially at high levels, though most products use 1% or less. Formulators treat it as a formulation problem fixable with xanthan gum or HPMC. Dimethicone is neither oil- nor water-soluble, which can contribute when layered — [SpecialChem, carbomer](https://www.specialchem.com/cosmetics/inci-ingredients/carbomer); [Evigrade, carbomer](https://www.evigrade.com/es/ingredients/carbomer). These are secondary or ingredient-database sources.
- **Sunscreen pilling (brand or practitioner blogs, weaker evidence):** pilling is more likely when there are too many layers, when a silicone sunscreen goes over a water-gel moisturiser, when sunscreen goes over heavy-residue products, or with excessive rubbing. Pilling can leave gaps in sun protection — [Odylique blog](https://odylique.co.uk/blog/why-does-sunscreen-pill/); [Dr Rachel Ho](https://www.drrachelho.com/blog/prevent-sunscreen-pilling/)

### Inferences
- The two chemists conflict on direction. Lab Muffin warns about any outer-phase mismatch; Ford says water primer under silicone foundation is usually fine. This suggests the base label is a weak predictor. Polymer and thickener content, the amount applied, and drying time probably matter at least as much.
- Pilling over sunscreen is a much bigger real-world problem than primer-foundation mismatch, based on the 2024 study. The finding that foundation "resolved" pilling could reflect its solvents re-wetting the layer. That is speculation; the authors propose only that it alters skin conditions.
- The study's sample was Chinese women in one setting, with brand-affiliated authors who appear to be from industry, so generalisation to other climates and skin types is uncertain.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study testing water-primer/silicone-foundation pairs against matched pairs.
- I found no primary writing from Chemist Confessions or Kind of Stephen on this question; searches didn't surface it.
- The AAD has no guidance on primer or foundation layering compatibility.

## Q3. Which base suits oily vs dry (and combination) skin, and why?

### Takeaway
The consensus is oily skin → oil-free, mattifying formulas (silicone-based or water-based with oil-absorbing powders such as silica, starch or clays, or powder foundations), and dry skin → hydrating formulas with humectants or emollient oils and fewer mattifying powders. The mechanisms are plausible, but the evidence is expert opinion plus industry sebum research, not comparative trials.

### Cited Findings
- **Oily-skin mechanism:** matte foundations use powders such as silica and zinc oxide that absorb oil, and mineral or powder makeup absorbs moisture. Dry skin should avoid "mattifying/oil-free" labels, which can dehydrate it, and look for glycerin, hyaluronic acid and ceramides. Oil-based foundations are aimed at dry skin — [Boots advice page](https://boots.com/skincare-beauty-advice/makeup-tutorials/face-and-foundation-tips/foundation-for-different-skin-types); [StyleVanity](https://stylevanity.com/2024/04/how-to-find-the-foundation-according-to-your-skin-type.html). These are retailer and blog sources, so evidence is low.
- **Silicone and skin type:** a dermatologist (Dr Hadley King, via a brand blog) prefers silicone foundations for oily skin because they're non-comedogenic, but says silicones can cling to dry flakes and emphasise them on dry skin — [PÜR Cosmetics blog quoting Dr Hadley King](https://purcosmetics.com/blogs/news/finding-the-right-foundation-for-your-skin-type-is-easy-with-the-help-of-award-winning-dermatologist-dr-hadley-king). This is a brand-hosted source.
- **Sebum mechanism (peer-reviewed):** sebum that mixes with foundation non-volatiles plasticises the film and increases transfer — [J. Cosmet. Sci. 68(1)](https://library.scconline.org/v068n01/94). Sebum is also the main cause of darkening — [SCC study](https://www.scconline.org/Portals/100/SCC-Study%20On%20the%20Factors%20Affecting%20The%20Darkening%20of%20liquid%20Foundations.pdf)
- **AAD (acne-prone or oily):** look for "oil-free", "non-comedogenic" or "won't clog pores". Powder foundations can give a light, oil-absorbing effect. Heavy liquid makeup that can trap oil may be worth avoiding. Salicylic-acid makeup is an option. Apply with clean brushes and wash them every 7–10 days — [AAD, acne and makeup](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/causes/makeup); [AAD makeup tips summary](https://www.biospace.com/makeup-tips-for-acne-prone-skin-american-academy-of-dermatology)
- **Dry skin and pilling (indirect):** in the 2024 study, people with lower hydration and lower sebum pilled more often — [PMC11294729](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11294729/)

### Inferences
- **Oily skin:** choose a sebum-immiscible film (silicone resins, volatile silicones) plus oil-absorbing fillers. Oil-based foundations are the worst fit mechanistically because sebum and the oils mix.
- **Dry skin:** emollient oils or humectant-rich water-based formulas sit better on rough skin. Very matte, high-powder or volatile-heavy silicone films can emphasise flakes.
- **Combination skin:** the logical approach is a satin water-in-silicone formula, or zone-specific powder or primer. This is an inference with no direct study.
- **Sensitive skin:** I found no evidence that one base is better. Fragrance, alcohol (denat.) and preservatives are more likely irritants than the base type. I found no source for this in this search, so treat it as a gap.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed comparison of foundation base types by skin type.
- I found no sensitive-skin evidence specific to foundation base.

## Q4. Are silicones or oils comedogenic or occlusive in a way that matters? How reliable are comedogenicity ratings?

### Takeaway
Dimethicone and other common silicones are generally considered non-comedogenic and only partly occlusive (vapour-permeable). Claims that they "clog pores" are mostly anecdotal. Ingredient comedogenicity ratings come largely from the rabbit-ear assay (1970s–80s), which over-predicts. Draelos & DiNardo (2006) showed that finished products containing "comedogenic" ingredients are not necessarily comedogenic. Concentration and vehicle matter more than the ingredient list.

### Cited Findings
- **History of the ratings:**
  - Kligman & Mills (1972) described "acne cosmetica".
  - Kligman & Kwong (1979) published an improved rabbit-ear model.
  - Mills & Kligman (1982) developed a human back model.
  - Fulton (1984) ran rabbit-ear testing of many ingredients and products.

  Rabbit-ear assays are faster but "less reliable" than human assays — [Acne.org](https://www.acne.org/what-is-comedogenicity-and-what-ingredients-are-comedogenic)
- **Draelos & DiNardo, J. Am. Acad. Dermatol. 2006, "A re-evaluation of the comedogenicity concept":** a modified human assay found that finished products made with "comedogenic" ingredients were not necessarily comedogenic. Ingredient-list screening can't account for concentration or vehicle — [North Biomedical summary](https://northbiomedical.com/articles/non-comedogenic-moisturizer/); [Skin Inc.](https://www.skininc.com/science/ingredients/article/21881078/comedogenicitya-complicated-conversation)
- **Lab Muffin** has a fact-check on using comedogenicity ratings — [Lab Muffin](https://labmuffin.com/fact-check-how-to-use-comedogenicity-ratings/). I could not retrieve its content, so it is not summarised here.
- **Dimethicone:** it is non-comedogenic and forms a vapour-permeable, not fully occlusive, barrier. It is rated about 1 on comedogenicity scales. Acne claims are "mostly anecdotal", and it appears in moisturisers made for acne-prone skin. Other ingredients in the same product can still clog pores — [UT Austin "Think Twice"](https://sites.utexas.edu/think-twice/2019/10/09/dimethicone/); [Curology](https://curology.com/blog/fact-or-fiction-is-dimethicone-in-skincare-products-bad-for-your-skin/)
- **Dermatologist's view:** silicones are "breathable" and non-comedogenic, though they could trap residue if skin isn't cleansed first. The article cites a 2021 safety assessment but gives no TEWL figures — [Dr Chalmers, Substack](https://drchalmers.substack.com/p/silicones-in-skincare-and-makeup). This is a secondary, weakly cited source.
- **AAD** still advises acne-prone patients to choose "oil-free/non-comedogenic" labelled makeup — [AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/causes/makeup). Note that "non-comedogenic" has no standardised FDA test definition. This is an inference consistent with the Draelos critique; I did not find a direct source in this search.

### Inferences
- "Oil-free" is a weak proxy. Many oils are low-risk in finished products, and some non-oil esters can score high on rabbit-ear lists. Patch-testing a finished product, or relying on human-tested "non-comedogenic" claims, is more meaningful than scanning ingredient charts.
- On a hot, humid day, the practical risk from silicone foundations is probably residue and incomplete removal, not inherent comedogenicity. The evidence is anecdotal or expert opinion.

### Gaps
- I found no TEWL data comparing dimethicone or cyclopentasiloxane films with oil-based foundation films.
- I found no controlled human comedogenicity study of whole foundations grouped by base type.
- I could not read the full text of Draelos & DiNardo 2006 here; the details come from secondary summaries.
