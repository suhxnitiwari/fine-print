# Silicone films beat oils in humid heat

Most liquid foundations sold today are not "water-based" in any meaningful sense. They are **water-in-silicone (W/Si) emulsions**: 40–60% water held as droplets inside a continuous silicone phase, so water is still ingredient #1 on the label ([UL Prospector](https://ulprospector.ul.com/knowledge/7389/pcc-contemporary-foundation-formulations/)). "Water-based", "silicone-based" and "oil-based" describe the **outer (continuous) phase**, and that phase, together with film-forming resins and oil-absorbing powders, decides wear, finish, transfer and how the product fails. Sebum is the main thing that breaks foundation down. It softens the film, increases transfer and darkens the shade ([J. Cosmet. Sci. 68(1)](https://library.scconline.org/v068n01/94)). Sebum mixes freely with an oil-continuous film, so oil-based foundations hold up worst on oily skin and in heat, where sebum output rises about **10% per 1 °C** of skin temperature ([J. Soc. Cosmet. Chem.](https://library.scconline.org/v024n05/69)). Silicone-resin long-wear formulas hold up best. You can read the base from an INCI list using the top five ingredients plus the emulsifier family, not ingredient #1, and the rules below are ready to code. For Austin summers and humid Indian seasons, the evidence supports **avoiding heavy leave-on oils and oil-based foundations** for oily, acne-prone, heavily sweating or heat-rash-prone skin. A flat "never use oil" rule is wrong, though: rinse-off cleansing oils, light hydrocarbons and C8/C10 lipids, and dry or air-conditioned skin are reasonable exceptions.

## Water-in-silicone emulsions replaced the old water-based foundation

Liquid foundations come in four architectures. **Oil-in-water (O/W)** puts oil droplets in water and is the true "water-based" type. **Water-in-oil (W/O)** puts water droplets inside an oil or hydrocarbon phase. **Water-in-silicone (W/Si)** does the same inside a silicone phase. **Anhydrous** products (sticks, pancake, waterless serum or oil foundations), usually defined in patents as under 5% water, make up the fourth ([UL Prospector](https://ulprospector.ul.com/knowledge/7389/pcc-contemporary-foundation-formulations/); [US 6,103,250](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6103250)). Formulators describe the "vast majority" of current liquid foundations as W/O or W/Si. The continuous oil or silicone phase touches the skin, so pigment spreads evenly without the dry, draggy feel it has in water, and better polymeric emulsifiers and pigment coatings made the format practical over the past 20 years ([UL Prospector](https://ulprospector.ul.com/knowledge/7389/pcc-contemporary-foundation-formulations/)). Cosmetic chemist Michelle Wong estimates W/Si at **over 90% of liquid foundations**. She attributes the shift to silicone's slip, its flexible long-wearing films, its non-greasy feel, and the fact that silicones let a brand print "oil-free" ([Lab Muffin](https://labmuffin.com/fixing-foundation-clumping-with-video/)). No independent market data backs the 90% figure, so treat it as an informed estimate.

A long-wear W/Si foundation is built in layers. A typical formula holds **40–60% water, 10–20% emollients/silicones, 3–15% TiO2 for coverage, 1–5% iron oxides for shade, and 2–5% emulsifier** ([UL Prospector](https://ulprospector.ul.com/knowledge/7389/pcc-contemporary-foundation-formulations/)). A volatile carrier, historically cyclopentasiloxane (D5), gives slip and then evaporates. Non-volatile dimethicone and elastomers stay behind for feel and blur. Silicone polyether emulsifiers such as PEG-10 dimethicone or cetyl PEG/PPG-10/1 dimethicone hold the water droplets in place. Resins such as **trimethylsiloxysilicate** form the flexible, rub-resistant, transfer-resistant film ([MakingCosmetics](https://makingcosmetics.com/on/demandware.static/-/Sites-makingcosmetics-master/default/dwa8b13674/fact-sheets/fact-sheet-silicone-resin.pdf)). Pigments are often coated with **triethoxycaprylylsilane**, a hydrophobic alkyl-silane that helps them disperse in the oil/silicone phase, absorb less oil, and stabilize W/Si and W/O emulsions ([Gelest](https://technical.gelest.com/brochures/cosmetic-pigments/triethoxycaprylylsilane-tc-treated/)). Many patents blend silicone and ester oils with both emulsifier types into a "water-in-silicone-and-oil" hybrid ([US 8,501,162](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8501162)).

Regulation is reshaping these labels. The EU banned D4 from cosmetics in 2019 and capped D4/D5 at 0.1% in wash-off products from January 2020 ([Ceway](https://news.ceway.eu/d4-d5-effectively-banned/); [Cosmetics & Toiletries](https://www.cosmeticsandtoiletries.com/regulations/regional/news/21841038/eu-effectively-bans-d4-and-d5-in-wash-off-products)). Regulation (EU) 2024/1328 extends a **0.1% cap on D5 and D6 to leave-on cosmetics from 6 June 2027** ([CosLaw.eu](https://coslaw.eu/reach-update-the-european-union-further-restricts-the-use-of-silicones-d5-and-d6-in-cosmetic-products/)). Sources disagree on whether D6 in rinse-off products falls under the cap in 2026 or 2027 ([Biorius](https://biorius.com/cosmetic-news/d4-d5-d6-restrictions/)). Formulators are replacing D5 with **isododecane, hemisqualane, C13-15 alkane and polydecene**, usually blended because no single material matches D5's evaporation rate ([Grand Ingredients](https://grandingredients.com/d5-replacement-siloxane-free/)). Silicone-free "green" foundations still use the W/O format, with squalane, caprylic/capric triglyceride, polyglyceryl emulsifiers and quaternium-18 or quaternium-90 clays ([UL Prospector](https://ulprospector.ul.com/knowledge/9591/pcc-how-to-formulate-green-liquid-foundations/)). As a result, "D5-free" no longer means "not silicone-based", and "silicone-free" no longer means "water-based".

## Sebum, not water, decides how each base wears

The best controlled data come from a Dow Corning study, not from comparisons of base labels. It found that long wear depends on the ratio of **"hard" non-volatiles** (resins, particulates) to **"soft" non-volatiles** (liquid oils, liquid emulsifiers). Too much soft material rubs off, and an all-hard film cracks. Pre-treating the substrate with artificial sebum **sharply increased pigment transfer in every formulation tested**, because sebum blends into the soft fraction and plasticizes the film. Films whose components were immiscible with sebum resisted it best ([J. Cosmet. Sci. 68(1)](https://library.scconline.org/v068n01/94)). L'Oréal's analysis of Double Wear, ColorStay and Teint Idole found that sebum and sweat performance depended on the resin or film former together with the filler ([ECI Digital Archives](https://dc.engconfintl.org/cosmetic/23)). An SCC study names sebum as the biggest cause of foundation darkening, ahead of sweat and light ([SCC](https://www.scconline.org/Portals/100/SCC-Study%20On%20the%20Factors%20Affecting%20The%20Darkening%20of%20liquid%20Foundations.pdf)).

From that mechanism, each base's behavior follows. No independent human trial has compared all three head to head, so the table below is a mechanistic synthesis anchored in the cited film-science work and expert consensus, not a measured ranking.

| Base (outer phase) | Wear and transfer | Typical finish | Oil control | Best skin fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| W/Si with resins (trimethylsiloxysilicate, silicone acrylates) | Longest; most transfer-resistant because the film resists sebum | Satin to matte (volatiles evaporate, powders remain) | Good, especially with silica or clay fillers | Oily, combination; can cling to dry flakes |
| W/O hydrocarbon (isododecane, C13-15 alkane, hemisqualane) | Close to W/Si: the carrier evaporates and leaves pigment and film | Dry to satin | Moderate to good | Oily to normal |
| O/W (water-continuous) | Thin film after water evaporates; wear depends on polymer film formers; less transfer resistance | Natural to dewy | Depends on added powders | Normal, dry, dehydrated |
| Oil-continuous or anhydrous oil | Least sebum-resistant; sebum mixes in and the film slides | Dewy, emollient | Poor | Dry, mature |

The skin-type advice in the table matches dermatologist and AAD guidance. Dr. Hadley King prefers silicone foundations for oily skin as non-comedogenic but notes they can **emphasize dry flakes** ([PÜR, quoting Dr. Hadley King](https://purcosmetics.com/blogs/news/finding-the-right-foundation-for-your-skin-type-is-easy-with-the-help-of-award-winning-dermatologist-dr-hadley-king)). The AAD advises oily and acne-prone skin to use "oil-free, water-based" and matte makeup ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/dry/oily-skin)). Note that in that AAD sense, "water-based" means "not oil-based"; it says nothing about the emulsion type. Brand "12-hour" and "24-hour" wear claims rest on panels of 20–33 people and are not independent evidence ([Mary Kay](https://www.marykay.com/klavorata/en-us/products/makeup/face/foundation/110351); [NARS HK](https://www.narscosmetics.com.hk/en/soft-matte-advanced-perfecting-powder/0194251136066_hk.html?cgid=face)).

Two popular beliefs hold up poorly. First, the "match water with water, silicone with silicone" layering rule is a simplification. Wong attributes clumping to mismatched outer phases ([Lab Muffin](https://labmuffin.com/fixing-foundation-clumping-with-video/)), but chemist Javon Ford reports that silicone foundations usually work fine over water-based primers ([Airmail](https://airmail.news/look/issues/2024-10-4/dont-be-a-pill)). In the largest study, 528 women, **80% of pilling happened when sunscreen went over a base product**, and rubbing caused more pilling than patting ([PMC11294729](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11294729/)). Second, silicones are not meaningful pore-cloggers. Dimethicone forms a vapor-permeable film and rates about 1 on comedogenicity scales ([UT Austin Think Twice](https://sites.utexas.edu/think-twice/2019/10/09/dimethicone/)). The ingredient comedogenicity scales themselves come from rabbit-ear assays, which Draelos and DiNardo showed do not predict how finished products behave ([Skin Inc.](https://www.skininc.com/science/ingredients/article/21881078/comedogenicitya-complicated-conversation)).

## The top five ingredients and the emulsifier reveal the base

Label law sets how much an INCI list can tell you. In both the US and the EU, ingredients **above 1% must appear in descending order**. Ingredients at or below 1% may follow in any order, and colorants (CI 77891 = TiO2; CI 77491/77492/77499 = iron oxides) may go at the end ([21 CFR 701.3](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-1996-title21-vol7/html/CFR-1996-title21-vol7-sec701-3.htm); [ChemLinked](https://cosmetic.chemlinked.com/cosmepedia/eu-cosmetic-ingredient-regulations)). Past roughly the first 5–8 ingredients, position therefore says almost nothing about concentration. W/Si foundations contain 40–60% water, so a water-first list does not mean the product is water-based ([NewBeauty](https://www.newbeauty.com/view/silicone-based-vs-water-based-makeup)). Wong's rule of thumb is that silicones in the top four of a foundation list make it "almost guaranteed" W/Si ([Lab Muffin](https://labmuffin.com/fixing-foundation-clumping-with-video/)). That rule needs three corrections. A silicone emulsifier can appear in waterless formulas ([US 6,103,250](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6103250)), so only the absence of water proves a product is anhydrous. Low-position silicones such as triethoxycaprylylsilane or methicone are usually pigment coatings ([Gelest](https://technical.gelest.com/brochures/cosmetic-pigments/triethoxycaprylylsilane-tc-treated/)), not evidence of the base. And post-2027 EU formulas will swap cyclic silicones for isododecane-type hydrocarbons, so a rule keyed only on "cyclopentasiloxane" will misfile them.

### Normalization and token families for the decoder

Before matching, lowercase the list and split it on commas. Drop everything after "may contain", "+/-" or "[+/-". Normalize "aqua/water/eau" to `water`. Set aside `ci \d{5}` tokens and named colorants (titanium dioxide, iron oxides, mica) as pigment information rather than phase evidence. Apply position logic only to the top eight tokens. The patterns below come from the emulsifier, stabilizer and carrier lists in the formulation sources ([UL Prospector](https://ulprospector.ul.com/knowledge/7389/pcc-contemporary-foundation-formulations/); [UL Prospector green](https://ulprospector.ul.com/knowledge/9591/pcc-how-to-formulate-green-liquid-foundations/); [US 8,501,162](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8501162); [Grand Ingredients](https://grandingredients.com/d5-replacement-siloxane-free/)). The O/W thickener list comes from general cosmetic-chemistry knowledge rather than a fetched source.

```python
FAMILIES = {
    "WATER":          r"^(water|aqua|eau)\b",
    "CYCLIC_SI":      r"cyclo(tetra|penta|hexa)siloxane|cyclomethicone",
    "SI_EMULSIFIER":  r"(peg|ppg|peg/ppg)-[\d/]+.*(dimethicone|methicone)|polydimethylsiloxyethyl dimethicone"
                      r"|tris\(trimethylsiloxy\)silylethyl dimethicone|dimethicone copolyol"
                      r"|peg-\d+ dimethicone crosspolymer|dimethicone/peg-[\d/]+ crosspolymer",
    "SI_FILM":        r"trimethylsiloxysilicate|polypropylsilsesquioxane|acrylates/dimethicone copolymer",
    "PIGMENT_COAT":   r"triethoxycaprylylsilane|isopropyl titanium triisostearate|hydrogenated lecithin",
    "OTHER_SI":       r"dimethicone|methicone|siloxane|silicate|silsesquioxane|trimethicone",  # after excluding above
    "VOLATILE_HC":    r"isododecane|isohexadecane|c\d+-\d+ (iso)?alkane|hemisqualane|undecane|tridecane|polydecene",
    "OIL_ESTER":      r"triglyceride|squalane|\boil\b|butter|jojoba|(palmitate|isononanoate|laurate|stearate|myristate)$",
    "WO_EMULSIFIER":  r"polyglyceryl-\d+ .*(stearate|polyhydroxystearate)|dipolyhydroxystearate|sorbitan (iso)?stearate"
                      r"|sorbitan oleate|quaternium-(18|90)|disteardimonium hectorite|stearalkonium hectorite",
    "OW_SIGNAL":      r"xanthan gum|carbomer|acrylates/c10-30 alkyl acrylate crosspolymer|acryloyldimethyltaurate"
                      r"|sodium polyacrylate|hydroxyethylcellulose|steareth-\d+|ceteareth-\d+|polysorbate \d+"
                      r"|peg-100 stearate|potassium cetyl phosphate",
}
```

### Decision order, first match wins

| Rule | Condition | Output | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | No WATER token anywhere (also check aloe juice or floral waters as water substitutes) | Anhydrous; subtype silicone vs. oil/hydrocarbon by majority of top 3 | High |
| R2 | WATER in top 3, a silicone (cyclic or other, excluding PIGMENT_COAT) in positions 1–4, and SI_EMULSIFIER present | W/Si ("silicone-based") | High |
| R3 | Position 1 is a silicone and water appears lower | W/Si (water under ~40%) | High |
| R4 | WATER in top 3, VOLATILE_HC or OIL_ESTER in positions 2–4, a W/O or Si emulsifier present, no silicone in top 4 | W/O hydrocarbon/oil (silicone-free) | Medium |
| R5 | Silicone and hydrocarbon/ester both in top 5, with SI_EMULSIFIER | W/Si-oil hybrid | Medium |
| R6 | WATER #1, no silicone or volatile hydrocarbon in top 4, no W/O emulsifier, and at least one OW_SIGNAL | O/W ("water-based") | High; "low" if no OW_SIGNAL |
| R7 | WATER #1, silicones in positions 2–5, no W/O emulsifier, O/W thickeners present | Si/W (water-continuous) | Speculative |
| R8 | Nothing matched | Unknown; return the evidence positions | n/a |

Several caveats should travel with every output. Silicones below position eight shouldn't flip an O/W verdict. A carrier that comes as a pre-blend (isododecane carrying trimethylsiloxysilicate, or dimethicone carrying dimethicone crosspolymer) ranks higher than its role suggests, but it still indicates the continuous phase. Glycerin or butylene glycol at position 2 shows up in every water-containing type and doesn't help classify. An "oil-free" claim usually signals silicone or hydrocarbon, not O/W. For layering and wear, R4 products behave more like W/Si than like O/W, so the decoder should never fold them into "water-based". None of these thresholds (water in the top 3, silicone in the top 4, an eight-token window) has been validated against a labeled dataset. Hand-label 50–100 foundations of known base and tune the rules against them before trusting the confidence scores.

## Humid heat does most of an oil's job for it

Humid summers change what skin needs. Sebum excretion climbs about 10% per 1 °C of skin temperature ([J. Soc. Cosmet. Chem.](https://library.scconline.org/v024n05/69)). Seasonal studies of Chinese and Korean women show cheek sebum and stratum-corneum hydration **peaking in summer and bottoming out in winter** ([PubMed 24602131](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24602131/); [CCID](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.2147/CCID.S286402?scroll=top)). In a muggy Austin July or an Indian monsoon, skin is already supplied with the water and lipids a leave-on oil is meant to provide, so the extra oil mostly adds shine, occlusion and a softer, more sebum-miscible makeup film. Dry heat is different. When Korean women spent six hours in 25 °C air below 20% humidity, their transepidermal water loss rose 22–27% and their facial sebum rose ([PMC10264749](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10264749/)). That supports some emollient use in hot-dry periods, such as pre-monsoon North India or West Texas, and in heavily air-conditioned rooms.

The clearest clinical harm from heavy oils in humid heat is **occlusion**, not "feeding" the skin. The MSD Manual states that miliaria (heat rash) arises when sweat is trapped in warm, humid weather and that "thick ointments should be avoided" ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/sweating-disorders/miliaria)). Malassezia folliculitis ("fungal acne") has hot, humid weather and follicular occlusion among its risk factors, and its management includes reducing occlusive products ([J Drugs Dermatol 2026](https://jddonline.com/malassezia-folliculitis-presentation-diagnosis-and-treatment-a-review-of-fungal-acne-2/)). The popular claim that certain oils feed fungal acne is weaker. Malassezia needs C11–C24 fatty acids to grow in culture. One in-vitro study ranked growth as butter > corn > olive > coconut > oleic acid > castor oil ([PMC10015641](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10015641/)), but no clinical trial shows that a facial oil causes fungal acne in people. Caprylic/capric (C8/C10) triglyceride falls outside the growth range, and mineral oil is non-digestible though still occlusive.

Oil type and use pattern matter more than the presence of oil. The AAD's oily-skin advice covers cleansers, makeup and sunscreen together: avoid oil-based cleansers, choose oil-free, non-comedogenic, water-based and matte makeup, cleanse after sweating, blot rather than rub, and avoid oily sunscreens ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/dry/oily-skin)). The only human data on cleansing oils come from a small cross-sectional study of 93 Indonesian students, in which cleansing-oil users had higher acne scores (14.1) than balm (9.3) or micellar-water users (7.7) ([MANUJU 2025](https://ejurnalmalahayati.ac.id/index.php/manuju/article/view/21210)). Skin type could easily confound that result. Even isopropyl myristate, a "notorious" ester, was not comedogenic at normal product concentrations ([J. Soc. Cosmet. Chem.](https://library.scconline.org/v040n06/20)). Sunscreen shows that "oily" and "fails in heat" are different things. Sweat can strip **83–99% of SPF** depending on the formula ([DTU / IJCS](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/242562258/ics.12703_2_.pdf)), and the most sweat-durable, FDA-tested 40- or 80-minute water-resistant formulas are often film-rich and can feel oily ([21 CFR 352.76](https://federal-regs.com/title/21/part-352/352.76/)). Under-application costs more protection than wash-off: SPF 70 applied at a realistic 1 mg/cm² delivered about SPF 26 ([J Drugs Dermatol 2018](https://jddonline.com/articles/dermatology/S1545961618P0116X)).

The answer for Austin and humid India follows from all this. Oily, combination, acne-prone or sweat-rash-prone skin should skip heavy plant oils, butters, petrolatum-type ointments and oil-based foundations as leave-ons. A resin-containing W/Si or hydrocarbon W/O foundation, oil-absorbing powder, a gel or fluid sunscreen reapplied after sweating, and blotting papers fit both the mechanism and AAD guidance. A cleansing oil or balm to remove long-wear makeup and water-resistant sunscreen is defensible when followed by a gentle water-based cleanse. Dry, mature or air-conditioning-dried skin can keep light lipids such as squalane or C8/C10 triglycerides. Two gaps remain. No IADVL guideline on summer or monsoon skincare turned up, and Indian advice to cleanse two to three times a day ([Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/weekend/perfect-skincare-tips-for-this-humid-weather)) conflicts with the AAD's "up to twice daily".

## Conclusion

The industry's labels hide the variable that actually predicts performance. Water-first ingredient lists, "oil-free" claims and EU D5 phase-outs all point away from the question that matters: **will sebum dissolve this film?** That explains why a silicone or isododecane W/O foundation behaves alike in heat while a "water-based" O/W and an oil-continuous product sit at opposite ends. It also means a decoder should report two signals: the outer phase, and whether resin film formers (trimethylsiloxysilicate, silicone acrylates) are present. Together they predict wear better than the base label alone.

For hot, humid climates, the more useful rule is "**avoid occlusive leave-on oils on oily or sweating skin**", not "no oil". It targets the documented harms, miliaria and occlusion-driven folliculitis, while keeping rinse-off oils and light lipids for skin that needs them. The weakest links are empirical. No independent trial has compared foundation bases under controlled heat and humidity, and no study has validated the label heuristics. A hand-labeled foundation dataset run through the rules above would test the decoder and help close that evidence gap.
